## Post-Entwürfe

### Entwurf 1

Job of the Week: Robotaxi Curb Marshal.

This week Tesla began paid rides in its steering-wheel-free Cybercab in Austin. In Las Vegas, Nevada has cleared Tesla, Waymo and Uber's robotaxi arm to run thousands of driverless cars, and each still needs the airport's own go-ahead before it can pick up there. Taxi groups told the hearing they worry about crowded roads around the airport and the Strip.

Now think about the last 50 metres of every trip. Who decides which car stops where? Who opens an extra bay when a concert lets out? Who calls the operator of a car that has frozen in the bus lane?

That could be a new job: the Curb Marshal. Part traffic choreographer, part negotiator between rival fleets, part crowd safety. Sci-fi has pictured stacked taxi lanes for decades, and The Fifth Element is a fun example. Someone has to run them.

Would you take this job?

### Entwurf 2

What does your job become? Nurse or carer edition.

Robots for older people now cost anywhere from about 250 dollars for a tabletop companion to around 25,000 dollars for a humanoid. A Beijing eldercare hub put 43 robots to work and pulled about 10 of them back for changes after feedback. A researcher told of Danish care homes where social robots ended up in cupboards.

The lesson I take from this: the machine is not the hard part. The fit is.

So I can see a nurse or carer growing into a Care Robot Matchmaker. Someone who spends two weeks in a person's real home, tries two or three robots, watches who talks to which one, and tells the family honestly which one suits, and when none does. It sounds a lot like the robot in Becky Chambers' A Psalm for the Wild-Built, who travels around asking people what they need.

The warm, human skill of noticing becomes the valuable one.

Would you want that work?

### Entwurf 3

Sci-fi saw it first: going underground.

In Silo, a whole society lives inside a buried structure, and an engineer keeps it alive. This week real data centres looked a little like that. A 100-metre-deep data centre inside an Italian quarry just finished construction, and one report counts another 120 US data centres under construction with 460 more planned. Meanwhile local resistance has blocked about 130 billion dollars of projects, according to one summary.

Two jobs follow, in my view. A Mine-to-Compute Planner, who reads the rock and decides which old mines can hold computers. And a Compute Commons Steward, who makes sure the neighbours get heat, water pledges and a share of the compute in return.

The pattern is the same one as always: a new machine, a new place, and new work around it. McKinsey's new US study this week also counts about 41 million jobs created against 36 million facing lower demand by 2035, though around 11 million people may need to change occupations.

Which job would you want in a Silo-style world?
